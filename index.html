Making Santa Barbara Work Better

Stay Safe SB is an independent community project focused on practical solutions to some of the challenges affecting the cost, safety and quality of life in Santa Barbara and communities throughout California.

Many of the problems we face are connected. Insurance affects the cost of owning and maintaining homes. Housing policy affects the willingness and ability of property owners to provide rental housing. Construction and permitting costs affect how much new housing can be built. Local operating costs affect the prices businesses must charge. And the availability of affordable workforce housing affects the people and institutions our community depends upon every day.

Our goal is not simply to identify problems. We want to develop practical policies that reward people for doing the right thing, target public assistance toward those who need it most, encourage investment in our community, and reduce unintended consequences.

1. Wildfire Risk and Homeowners Insurance

California homeowners in high-fire-risk areas have experienced dramatic increases in the cost and availability of insurance. At the same time, the condition of properties in these areas can affect the safety of the larger community.

Homes at the edge of wildland areas can effectively become part of the community's first line of defense. Vegetation management, fire-resistant construction, defensible space and property maintenance can help reduce the likelihood that wildfire spreads from one property to another.

For that reason, insurance policy should strongly encourage homeowners to make meaningful improvements.

The problem with all-or-nothing standards

Current wildfire mitigation programs can sometimes operate like an on/off switch. A property must satisfy an entire set of requirements before receiving a particular designation or benefit.

That approach can discourage homeowners who have completed most important mitigation measures but cannot reasonably satisfy one particular requirement.

A real-world example: A homeowner may have a hot tub located approximately 30 feet from the house, but a combustible deck surrounding the hot tub may prevent the property from satisfying a mitigation standard. Meanwhile, another property configuration may satisfy the technical standard even though a neighboring structure or combustible feature is physically much closer. When homeowners believe that one condition eliminates the value of every other improvement they have made, the incentive to continue investing in mitigation can disappear.

A better approach

California should consider a graduated mitigation system that recognizes incremental risk reduction.

Instead of receiving zero benefit until every requirement is satisfied, homeowners could receive increasing insurance credits or underwriting benefits as meaningful mitigation measures are completed.

That creates an incentive for everyone to improve their property—even when achieving a perfect standard is impossible.

2. Housing Policy and Rent Stabilization

Santa Barbara faces a genuine housing affordability problem. Renters, particularly members of the local workforce, can struggle to afford housing near where they work.

But housing policies also need to recognize the rapidly increasing costs of providing and maintaining rental housing.

Property insurance, utilities, repairs, labor, appliances, property taxes, maintenance, gardening and turnover expenses can increase much faster than general rent limits.

For example, one local rental property's annual insurance premium recently increased from approximately $7,000 to $12,000 in one year. That is an increase of roughly 71 percent in a single expense category.

When operating costs rise substantially faster than permitted rent increases, property owners may respond in predictable ways.

Utilities that were once included in rent may begin to be billed separately. Annual rent increases may become automatic rather than occasional. Owners may defer optional upgrades or amenities. Rental properties may also become less attractive as long-term investments.

Good housing policy should consider these incentives rather than assume property operating costs remain constant.

3. Assistance Should Reach the People Who Need It

One important question in housing policy is whether assistance should primarily reduce rents for every renter or whether public resources should be targeted toward households most affected by housing costs.

Broad rent regulations can benefit renters across a wide range of incomes, including households capable of paying market rents.

Targeted affordable housing programs take a different approach. They use public resources to provide housing specifically for residents whose income makes Santa Barbara difficult to afford.

Supporting workforce housing

Teachers, caregivers, hospitality workers, maintenance employees, service workers, public employees and many others perform jobs our community depends upon.

Programs that create housing specifically for lower- and moderate-income residents can allow these workers to live closer to their jobs and remain part of the community they serve.

Stay Safe SB wants to highlight successful programs and organizations that are actually adding affordable units to the local housing supply.

4. The Hidden Cost of Living in Santa Barbara

Housing costs affect more than housing.

Businesses operating in high-cost communities face higher commercial rents, labor costs, insurance expenses and other operating costs. Those expenses can ultimately appear in the price of everyday goods and services.

The same restaurant chain, retailer or service provider can therefore charge different prices in different communities.

A sandwich, meal or other everyday purchase in Santa Barbara can cost more than the same item sold by the same company in a lower-cost nearby community. The difference may seem small on a single purchase, but the cumulative effect contributes to the overall cost of living.

Housing policy, commercial rents, wages, construction costs and local regulations are therefore interconnected.

5. Building More Housing Should Not Take Years

Nearly everyone agrees that California needs more housing. But building housing can be extraordinarily expensive and time-consuming.

Permitting, design review, engineering requirements, building codes, environmental requirements, fees and multiple layers of approval can add significant cost and delay to a project.

Some requirements are important for safety, environmental protection and neighborhood quality. But the system should also ask a basic question:

If we want more housing, are our rules making it unnecessarily difficult or expensive to create it?

Improving the permitting process does not require eliminating safety standards. It can mean clearer rules, predictable timelines, better coordination between departments and faster decisions.

Our Guiding Principles

  • Reward improvement. Policies should encourage people to make their homes, businesses and communities safer and better.
  • Avoid all-or-nothing incentives. Partial progress that meaningfully reduces risk should still have value.
  • Target assistance where it is most needed. Public resources should prioritize people facing genuine financial barriers.
  • Consider unintended consequences. Rules can change behavior. Policymakers should consider how residents, property owners and businesses are likely to respond.
  • Encourage housing supply. Policies should make it easier—not unnecessarily harder—to create and maintain good housing.
  • Use real-world data. Policy should be evaluated based on actual costs, outcomes and community impact.
  • Keep the conversation constructive. Renters, homeowners, property owners, businesses, workers and local government all have legitimate interests in the future of our community.
